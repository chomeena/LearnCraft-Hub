📄 Resume Building

A resume that gets past the bots
— and wins over humans.

Most resumes are rejected by ATS software before a human ever sees them. Here's how to fix that — and how to stand out once you do get read.

✅ ATS Survival Guide
01
Single-column layout only. No tables, columns, or text boxes — ATS parsers read left to right, line by line, and anything else confuses them.
02
Mirror the job description. Copy exact keywords from the posting into your skills and experience — don't paraphrase them.
03
Standard section headings. Use "Experience", "Education", "Skills", "Projects" — never creative labels like "My Journey".
04
Text-based PDF or .docx only. Never submit a scanned image, photo, or Canva design exported as PDF.
05
Quantify everything possible. "Improved load time by 40%", "Dashboard used by 500 users" — numbers catch both ATS and human eyes.
06
Spell out acronyms fully. Write "JavaScript" not "JS", "Machine Learning" not "ML" — ATS systems match exact strings.
07
Contact info in the body. Some ATS systems skip content placed in Word headers or footers — put your name and links in the actual document body.
08
One page max under 2 years. Recruiters spend 7 seconds on a first pass — longer is not better, it's harder to scan.

30-Day Plan
What actually happens in a tech interview?
Most students go in blind. Here's the exact sequence every major IT company in India follows — so you know exactly what's coming.
1
Online Assessment (OA)
Aptitude + coding (2–3 problems). Platforms: HackerRank, Codility, Mettl, AMCAT. Duration: 60–120 mins. Tip: solve easy/medium DSA + practice verbal reasoning a week before.
AptitudeDSA (Easy)Verbal Reasoning
2
Technical Round 1 — DSA & Fundamentals
Live coding on shared IDE + CS concept questions. 45–60 mins. They want to see your process, not just the answer. Always think out loud.
Arrays / StringsOOPDBMS basicsOS concepts
3
Technical Round 2 — Projects & System Design
Deep dive into your resume projects + basic system design (for experienced freshers). 45 mins. Have a story ready for every project listed — problem, decision, challenge, result.
Project walkthroughTech stack reasoningLow-level design
4
HR Round — Fit & Offer
Motivation, communication, and culture fit. 20–30 mins. This is eliminatory — candidates who don't prepare get rejected here. Research the company before this round.
STAR storiesSalary negotiationCulture fit

OA → TR1 → HR
Heavy on aptitude. OA is the main filter. TR is moderate DSA + DBMS/OS fundamentals.
Zoho / Freshworks
OA → TR1 → TR2 → HR
Zoho is famous for programming-heavy rounds. Freshworks focuses heavily on projects + culture fit.
Startups (Series A–C)

Practical over theoretical. Show working code. Culture fit matters as much as skill.
Product Companies (mid-tier)

5–6 rounds. More depth. Expect system design even as a fresher. Portfolio is critical.
HR rounds eliminate more candidates than coding rounds do.
Preparation takes 2 hours. Most people skip it. Don't be most people.
⭐ The STAR Framework — use this for every behavioural question
S
Situation — set the scene briefly. One sentence max. Who, what, where.
T
Task — what was your specific responsibility? What did you own?
A
Action — what did YOU do? Be specific. Avoid "we". Own your contribution.
R
Result — what changed? Quantify if possible. What did you learn?
Tell me about yourself
Past → Present → Future (90 sec max)
Past: who you are, what you studied, what you've built. Present: what skills you're actively developing right now. Future: why this specific role at this company excites you. End with energy — don't trail off.
❌ Don't: read your resume aloud. They have it. Tell a story.
Why do you want to work here?
Research → Connect → Contribute
Name one specific thing you found — a product, a tech blog post, a recent launch. Then connect it to something you've built or learned. Then say how you'd contribute. Generic answers ("great company, good culture") are automatic red flags.
❌ Don't: say "good growth opportunities" or "I like the brand".
Describe a time you failed
STAR + Lesson (the lesson is the point)
Pick a real failure — not a disguised strength. The interviewer is measuring self-awareness and growth mindset. Spend 40% of your answer on what you changed as a result. A candidate who owns mistakes and learned from them is trusted more than one who seems perfect.
❌ Don't: say "I'm a perfectionist" or "I work too hard".
Where do you see yourself in 5 years?
Show ambition, not job-hopping
Talk about depth, not titles. "I want to become someone who deeply understands [their domain] and can make real product decisions." Mention skills you want to grow, not companies you plan to leave for. Tie your answer back to this company's growth area.
❌ Don't: say you want to start your own company in 2 years.
What are your salary expectations?
Research first. Give a range. Never anchor low.
Research on LinkedIn Salary, Glassdoor, AmbitionBox for that exact role + city. Give a range: "Based on my research I'm targeting ₹X–Y LPA, though I'm open to the full package." If you anchor too low you can't negotiate up. If too high without justification, it signals poor research.
❌ Don't: say "whatever you think is fair". It signals zero confidence.
Do you have any questions for us?
Always have 2 good questions ready
Ask about: (1) what the onboarding looks like in the first 90 days, (2) what the current team's biggest technical challenge is, (3) how success is measured in this role. Good questions show genuine interest and business thinking. They also help you evaluate if this job is right for you.
❌ Don't: ask about salary, leave policy, or working hours in the first round.
Technical interviews test how you think, not just what you know.
Talking through your process out loud is worth more than getting the right answer silently.

🔧 CS Fundamentals — Frequently Tested
🗄️
DBMS Essentials
Asked in nearly every fresher round
▾
ACID properties
Atomicity — all or nothing. Consistency — data stays valid. Isolation — concurrent transactions don't interfere. Durability — committed data survives crashes. Give a banking example: a transfer deducts from A and credits B, or neither happens.
INNER vs LEFT JOIN
INNER: only rows where the condition matches in both tables. LEFT JOIN: all rows from the left table, with NULLs where the right table has no match. Draw it as a Venn diagram if asked — interviewers love that you can visualise it.
Indexing explained simply
An index is a sorted copy of one column (or more) that makes lookups O(log n) instead of O(n). Tradeoff: reads are faster, writes are slightly slower because the index must also be updated. Composite indexes match on the leftmost prefix of columns.
Normalization (1NF–3NF)
1NF: atomic values, no repeating groups. 2NF: no partial dependency (each non-key column depends on the whole key). 3NF: no transitive dependency (non-key columns don't depend on other non-key columns). Denormalize for read-heavy workloads.
⚙️
OS Concepts
TCS, Infosys, and Wipro love these
▾
Process vs Thread
A process is an independent program in execution with its own memory space. A thread is a lightweight unit within a process that shares memory with its siblings. Threads are faster to create and switch between. Analogy: a process is a restaurant; threads are the waiters sharing the same kitchen.
Deadlock — 4 conditions
Deadlock needs ALL four: Mutual exclusion (resource held by one process at a time), Hold and wait (process holds a resource while requesting more), No preemption (resources can't be forcefully taken), Circular wait (chain of processes each waiting on the next).
Virtual Memory
Lets a process use more memory than physically available by storing pages on disk and bringing them to RAM on demand (page fault). It creates the illusion of a large private memory space. Swap space on disk acts as the overflow. Too many page faults → thrashing (system slows to a crawl).
CPU Scheduling Algorithms
FCFS — first come first served (convoy effect). SJF — shortest job first (optimal avg wait, but starvation). Round Robin — time quantum each (fair, used in real OS). Priority Scheduling — highest priority first. For interviews: know the tradeoffs, not just names.
🌐
Networking & Web
Must-know for Full Stack, Cloud, and Security roles
▾
What happens when you type a URL?
DNS lookup → TCP handshake → TLS handshake (HTTPS) → HTTP request sent → Server processes → Response with HTML → Browser parses HTML → CSS + JS loaded → Page rendered. Mention caching at DNS and CDN level for bonus points.
TCP vs UDP
TCP: reliable, ordered, connection-based. Uses for: web, email, file transfer. UDP: fast, connectionless, no guarantee. Uses for: video calls, gaming, DNS queries. The tradeoff is reliability vs speed. VoIP uses UDP because a dropped packet is better than a delayed one.
REST API — the 5 methods
GET — read. POST — create. PUT — replace. PATCH — partial update. DELETE — remove. Know common status codes: 200 OK, 201 Created, 400 Bad Request, 401 Unauthorized, 403 Forbidden, 404 Not Found, 500 Server Error.
HTTP vs HTTPS
HTTP transmits data as plain text — anyone intercepting the network can read it. HTTPS uses TLS encryption — data is encrypted in transit. The browser negotiates a session key using asymmetric encryption (RSA/ECC), then switches to symmetric encryption (AES) for speed.
🖥️ How to Handle Live Coding Rounds
1
Repeat & clarify — Restate the problem in your own words. Ask about edge cases (empty input? negative numbers? very large n?). Never assume. This alone sets you apart from 80% of candidates.
2
Think out loud first — Say "My initial approach would be X because..." before typing a single character. Interviewers grade your reasoning process, not just the final code.
3
Start brute force, then optimise — Mention the naive O(n²) solution first, then improve it. A working slow solution scores higher than an unfinished fast one.
4
Write clean code — Meaningful variable names. Brief comments on tricky logic. Most interviewers value readable code over clever one-liners.
5
Test with examples — After writing the solution, trace through a sample input manually. Catch bugs before they do. Then mention time & space complexity unprompted.
6
When stuck, say so — "I'm not immediately sure of the optimal approach — let me think about what data structure would help here." Silence looks like confusion. Thinking out loud looks like a collaborator.

Communication skills are assessed from the moment you join the call. Here's how to get them right.
😤
Managing Nerves
1Before the interview: box breathe — 4 counts in, hold 4, out 4. Repeat 3 times. It activates the parasympathetic nervous system.
2Slow your speech by 20%. Nervous people rush. A deliberate pace signals confidence to the interviewer, even if you don't feel it.
3A 3-second pause before answering signals thoughtfulness, not confusion. Train yourself to pause before speaking — not after a silence panic-fills you.
🎙️
Eliminating Filler Words
1Record yourself answering "Tell me about yourself." Most people are shocked by how many "um", "like", and "you know" they use. Count them. Target 0.
2Replace every filler with silence. It feels uncomfortable — but to the listener, a brief pause sounds composed and intentional.
3Practice answering 5 questions aloud every day for 7 days. Fillers are a habit — they disappear with conscious repetition and self-observation.
📷
Virtual Interview Setup
1Camera at eye level, not below. Below-eye camera adds a power imbalance that reads as unconfident. Stack books under your laptop if needed.
2Look at the camera lens, not at their face on screen. That's what "eye contact" looks like through a webcam. It feels unnatural but looks natural to them.
3Light from the front, not behind you. A window behind you makes your face dark and shadowed. A lamp facing you from in front makes you look alert and present.
🗂️
Structuring Your Answers
1Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF): give the answer in one sentence first, then explain. Interviewers follow this better than long wind-ups to a conclusion.
2For technical explanations: use an analogy first, then technical detail. "Think of it like a restaurant kitchen — threads are the chefs sharing one set of equipment."
3Keep answers to 60–90 seconds unless asked to elaborate. Long answers signal poor prioritisation. Short, crisp answers with depth on follow-up show mastery.
📹 The Self-Recording Drill (Do this once a week)
Step 1Record yourself answering "Tell me about yourself" — no do-overs, one take.
Step 2Watch it back with the sound off first. Count fidgets, eye contact breaks, posture shifts.
Step 3Watch it again with sound. Count filler words. Write the number down.
Step 4Record again. Compare. After 3 sessions, most people cut fillers by 70%.

Evaluators are watching for leadership, listening, and the ability to build — not win — an argument.
🎯 What Evaluators Are Actually Scoring
Content Quality
Facts, structure, relevance of points
Communication
Clarity, tone, body language
Leadership
Initiating, summarising, including others
Listening
Building on others' points, not talking over
Team Behavior
Respecting others, not dismissing ideas
💬 Moves That Win GDs
Opening
Listen for 30–60 seconds before speaking. Your first point should be more informed than anyone else's because you listened first. Open with a fact or a clear framework.
Building
Use "Yes, and..." instead of "But". Acknowledge the previous point: "That's a strong point — I'd add that..." It shows you're listening AND contributing.
Disagreeing
"I see that perspective — from a different angle though..." or "That works in X context, but in Y context..." Never "No, that's wrong." Evaluators penalise dismissiveness heavily.
Leadership
Invite a quiet participant: "We haven't heard from [name] — what's your take?" Then offer to summarise: "Let me capture where we've landed..." Both score high for leadership.
Closing
Offer a synthesis when the group runs out of steam: "To summarise the key points raised so far: [A], [B], and [C] — and I think the crux of the disagreement is [X]." This is a high-value move.
